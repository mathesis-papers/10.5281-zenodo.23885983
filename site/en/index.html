Resolving User3's orthogonal absolute-product question: minimum and minimizers

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

How small can a matrix-based measure become when one changes orthonormal coordinates, after discarding the signs that normally allow cancellation? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang show that its minimum is always exactly one, characterize every coordinate change achieving it, and separately resolve a related question about making row sizes equal.

Fix an invertible real K-by-K matrix P, with K positive. An orthogonal matrix Q preserves lengths and angles, and its inverse is its transpose. Replace every entry of PQ and of its inverse by its absolute value, then multiply: M = |PQ| |QᵀP⁻¹|. Its spectral radius ρ(M) is the largest absolute value of its eigenvalues. User3 asked whether some P could force this quantity uniformly above one for every Q, and what its infimum is. The authors prove that the minimum is one for every P and is attained.

The lower bound comes from cancellation: before taking absolute values, the factors multiply to the identity. The triangle inequality makes every diagonal entry of M at least one. Their sum is at least K, so the largest eigenvalue magnitude cannot be below one. To attain the bound, orthogonalize P’s rows in order, subtracting projections onto previously obtained directions and normalizing. Taking these directions as Q’s columns makes PQ lower triangular, meaning its entries above the diagonal vanish. Its inverse is also lower triangular. Their absolute product has diagonal entries—and therefore eigenvalues—all equal to one. Column-sign changes preserve the objective, allowing Q also to preserve orientation.

The authors’ classification says that an invertible A satisfies ρ(|A| |A⁻¹|) = 1 exactly when independent row and column reorderings make A lower triangular. Equality forces all eigenvalues of the absolute product to be one. Subtracting the identity gives a nonnegative matrix whose sufficiently high powers vanish. Viewing positive entries as directed edges, there can be no cycle: repeated traversal would keep some powers nonzero. Ordering this acyclic graph triangularizes the product; its diagonal contributions then identify distinct pivot columns that triangularize A. Thus every minimizing Q comes from orthogonalizing some ordering of P’s rows, followed by column reorderings and sign changes. There are at most 2ᴷ(K!)² distinct minimizers; the description can contain repetitions.

A small example explains why attaining one does not mean obtaining the identity. In the authors’ rational two-dimensional example, their rotation turns P into the matrix with rows (1,0) and (1,1). Its inverse has rows (1,0) and (−1,1). Taking absolute values and multiplying gives rows (1,0) and (2,1). Both eigenvalues are one, although the matrix is neither the identity nor diagonalizable. Triangular structure, rather than elimination of all off-diagonal entries, explains the minimum.

The connection to numerical conditioning comes from S. M. Rump’s earlier scaling theorem. It identifies this spectral radius with the infimum of the infinity-norm condition number under positive diagonal row and column scalings. That condition number multiplies the largest absolute row sum of a matrix by the corresponding quantity for its inverse. Attainment matters: for the triangular example, the relevant single-scaling expression is 1 + 2d₁/d₂, with positive factors d₁ and d₂. It approaches one but never equals it, unlike the attained orthogonal minimum.

The companion question asks whether an orthogonal change can equalize the inverse factor’s absolute row sums. The authors answer yes for every invertible real L, not merely lower-triangular L, again allowing orientation preservation. Yamabe and Yujobo’s theorem gives an orthonormal basis on which a continuous function on the unit sphere has equal values. Apply it to the sum of absolute coordinates of uᵀL⁻¹; invertibility makes the common value positive. This settles exact balancing and hence the weaker approximate request. However, balancing need not minimize the spectral objective: for the diagonal matrix with entries 1 and 2, every balancing transformation gives spectral radius two, whereas the minimum is one.

The authors credit the triangularization argument to the cited Omega Institute Lean source. As described in the manuscript, that formal result covers the universal lower bound and existence of an attaining orthogonal matrix. The classification, orientation-preserving refinement and balancing conclusion receive written proofs here. The mentioned rational-arithmetic check program must be distinguished from these general proofs; its availability does not establish that checks were executed. Neither original question remains conjectural within the finite-dimensional, invertible real setting. The contribution is an attained exact minimum with a complete minimizer description, alongside a separate balancing theorem—not a guarantee that both objectives can be achieved together.
